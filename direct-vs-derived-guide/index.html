Direct vs Derived Guide

Tell the source wording apart from the note you write from it, so a paraphrase never quietly upgrades into a new fact.

Two kinds of text

Every read you make contains two kinds of text: what the source directly shows, and what you write about it. Keeping them apart is the whole point of the balance.

Directly visible

The source’s own wording — a title, an option name, a price, a domain. You can point at it. It does not need your help to be what it is.

Derived note

Your working wording — a summary, a label, or a guess about what the source probably means. It is useful while you work, but it is yours, not the source’s.

Do not upgrade a paraphrase

A derived note becomes a problem the moment you treat it as the source’s wording. If your note says “the price is per item,” that is still an inference until the source states it.

A worked split

  1. Directly visible: the page shows “$45” next to the bag.
  2. Derived note: “the price is probably for one bag.”
  3. Check: does the source say “per bag”? It does not — the note stays derived.

Context example — not a product score, comparison result, recommendation, or verification.

When the note keeps replacing the source

If you find yourself quoting your note instead of the source, stop and re-read the page. The source wording is the only thing that can carry the claim.
